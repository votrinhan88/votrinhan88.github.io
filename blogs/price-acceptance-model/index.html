Price Acceptance Model

Created on July 27, 2026

2026   ·   pricing   modeling   statistics   ·   ml

Recently made a new friend in an AI meetup, let’s call him Jaron. Jaron manages a short-term apartment rentals business. He wants to maximize revenue by determining the highest price possible that a customer is willing to pay for an apartment rent. He was also intrigued by several what-ifs: how much should he lower the price to get that missed customer, how much more he could have earned from that secured one, or what feature of an apartment can significantly drive up the price, etc. I took the problem home, and spent some time on it as a brain exercise.

Assumptions

Before writing down a model, it helps to setup what the data actually looks like and what we’re modeling.

The data. I assume Jaron would have historical booking attempts: each one is a $(\text{price}, \text{apartment}, \text{requirements})$ triplet, along with its outcome (accept/reject).

The target. We want to model the probability that a booking is accepted, given a price and some features of the apartment and the customer’s requirements:

\[P(\text{accept} \mid \text{price}, \text{apartment}, \text{requirements}).\]

We’d also want to be able to generalize to feature combinations the data never showed us, say, this apartment has one more bedroom, and still get something sensible back. It should also be smooth in price, since finding the best price later means differentiating with respect to it.

Apartments repeat frequently, customers don’t. A typical apartment shows up in many booking attempts, so it’s feasible, and worthwhile, to learn an apartment-specific price-response curve. A typical customer, by contrast, is mostly one-off: they search for an apartment only once in a while, and within a single trip their requirements don’t vary much. So rather than modeling who the customer is, I represent them only through trip/session features (dates, party size, lead time, etc.).

The data is sparse. Because customers rarely repeat, most apartment-requirement combinations are seen only a handful of times. That pushes us toward a simpler model, one that resists overfitting and stays interpretable and tractable.

Modeling

Those constraints point to a linear combination of features inside a sigmoid: it is smooth in $p$, generalizes past what we’ve observed, and keeps every coefficient readable as an effect on log-odds. With data this sparse, that simplicity beats raw expressiveness.

Let us formalize a booking attempt, which requests apartment $j \in {1, \ldots, M}$ at price $p$ and is described by:

  • $p$: the price quoted
  • $\mathbf{x}$: customer requirement features (party size, length of stay, lead time, day-of-week, device, trip purpose if inferable, search/session behavior, etc.)
  • $\mathbf{z}_j$: apartment features (apartment type, capacity, amenities, location, etc.)
  • $\mathbf{m}_j$: the “matchness” between $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}_j)$ (*), features shared between $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{z}_j$ that reflect how well the customer’s requirements match the apartment’s available features.

(*) These matchness features arguably deserve their own sub-problem on learning a probabilistic model. To stay focused on our main problem, let’s assume \(\mathbf{m}_j\) is already available as an input feature by the time this model runs, however it gets estimated. One naive way to get there is to hand-write a rule per requirement, like \(m_\text{capacity} = \mathbf{1}[z_\text{capacity} \ge x_\text{party_size}]\): cheap, interpretable, and good enough whenever the requirement is a clean threshold. For softer matches, like amenities the customer wants versus what the apartment has, a hand-picked rule is harder to justify, so a more methodical option is to estimate the match empirically. From historical accepted attempts, measure how often each required-versus-provided amenity combination actually shows up, and use that observed rate as the match weight instead of guessing one.

Putting together, the price acceptance model predicts the acceptance probability of a booking attempt given price $p$, requirements $\mathbf{x}$, and apartment $j$ (which fixes $\mathbf{z}_j$ and, together with $\mathbf{x}$, the matchness $\mathbf{m}_j$) as:

\[P(\text{accept} \mid p, \mathbf{x}, j) = \text{sigmoid}\left( (\alpha + \alpha_j) + (\beta + \beta_j) \cdot p + {\theta_\mathbf{x}}^\top \mathbf{x} + {\theta_\mathbf{z}}^\top \mathbf{z}_j + {\theta_\mathbf{m}}^\top \mathbf{m}_j \right),\]

where the learnable parameters are:

  • $\alpha, \alpha_j$: the global desirability and its apartment-specific deviation.
  • $\beta, \beta_j$: the global price-sensitivity and its apartment-specific deviation.
  • $\theta_\mathbf{x}, \theta_\mathbf{z}, \theta_\mathbf{m}$: effects of the requirements, apartment, and matchness features

The apartment-specific features $\alpha_j, \beta_j$ are my addition to model the response of each apartment to price. This can be valuable when we want to diagnose a specific apartment, e.g., why it might be under-/over-performing compared to the population, or if there’s any missed feature that our model hasn’t factored in. In principle, apartments show up frequently in the data, so we can trivially deduce $\alpha_j, \beta_j$ as the residues from the population’s values $\alpha, \beta$. However, fitting $\alpha_j, \beta_j$ independently for each apartment makes them vulnerable to overfitting on low-data entries.

Thus, I propose we subject $\alpha_j, \beta_j$ to a Gaussian prior centered at zero $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$. Such prior shrinks $\alpha_j, \beta_j$ toward zero by default. In low-data cases, that shrinkage wins: an apartment with only a handful of bookings gets pulled back near zero, so its deviation barely affects its own predictions. A large deviation has to be earned, through enough booking attempts that $\alpha_j, \beta_j$ are correctly calibrated. This is actually regularization, but only on $\alpha_j, \beta_j$, and its strength is fitted from the data instead of hand-picked.

Formally, each apartment’s intercept deviation $\alpha_j$ and price-slope deviation $\beta_j$ are draws from that shared prior:

\[\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j \\ \beta_j\end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma), \qquad \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\sigma_\alpha^2 & \rho\sigma_\alpha \sigma_\beta \\ \rho \sigma_\alpha \sigma_\beta & \sigma_\beta^2\end{pmatrix},\]

where $\alpha_j$ and $\beta_j$ are allowed to correlate through $\rho$, since highly-desired apartments might also turn out to be less price-sensitive. $\sigma_\alpha$, $\sigma_\beta$, and $\rho$ aren’t fixed by hand either: they’re estimated from the data alongside $\alpha_j$ and $\beta_j$ themselves, setting how strongly that pull toward zero is felt population-wide.

Only $\alpha$ and $\beta$ get this apartment-level pooling. $\theta_\mathbf{x}, \theta_\mathbf{z}, \theta_\mathbf{m}$ stay fixed and shared across all apartments, since there isn’t enough per-apartment data to estimate how a feature’s, requirement’s, or matchness’s effect varies apartment by apartment. The result: apartments with thin history lean on the population curve, well-observed ones get their own distinct curve, and the balance between the two is set by the data rather than a hand-picked hyperparameter.

Fitting

To fit our features, we want to maximize the probability that the observed data would occur under our chosen parameters, i.e., the likelihood. The likelihood function takes the same formula as the probability itself, just read as a function of the parameters while data held fixed:

\[\ell(\text{params} \mid \text{data}) = P(\text{data}; \text{params}).\]

To simplify our derivation, let us denote $X = (p, \mathbf{x}, j)$ for inputs to a booking attempt, and $\Theta = (\alpha, \beta, \theta_\mathbf{x}, \theta_\mathbf{z}, \theta_\mathbf{m}, \alpha_j, \beta_j)$ for every parameter in the model. Let’s also define $y \in {0, 1}$ (0 = reject, 1 = accept) for the booking attempt’s outcome, so $P(y=1 \mid X;\Theta)$ is exactly the acceptance probability our model already computes. Since $y$ only takes two values and we’ve fixed $P(y=1\mid X;\Theta)$, it follows a Bernoulli distribution by definition, and its likelihood is:

\[\ell(\Theta \mid y, X) = P(y=1 \mid X; \Theta)^{y} \, \big(1 - P(y=1 \mid X; \Theta)\big)^{1-y}.\]

The formula collapses to $P(y=1 \mid X; \Theta)$ when $y=1$ and to $1 - P(y=1 \mid X; \Theta)$ when $y=0$. Now we index attempts by $i=1, \dots, N$ and under the assumption that they’re i.i.d. The full-dataset likelihood is a product over every attempt:

\[L(\Theta) = \prod_{i=1}^N \ell(\Theta \mid y_i, X_i).\]

Which is terribly ugly to deal with, so in practice, we usually deal with the log-likelihood variation to strip down a product to a sum. Also, by ML convention, we also flip the sign, turning a maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) problem into a minimization problem of the negative log-likelihood (NLL). “Why?” you might ask, I reckon it reads as a cost for convenience, and most optimization techniques are minimization by default.

\[\text{NLL}(\Theta) = -\log L(\Theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^N \log \ell(\Theta \mid y_i, X_i).\]

Now we would like to plug $\ell(\Theta \mid y, X)$ into $\text{NLL}(\Theta)$ to fully populate our parameters $X$:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{NLL}(\Theta) &= -\sum_{i=1}^N \log \left[ P(y=1 \mid X; \Theta)^{y} \, \big(1 - P(y=1 \mid X; \Theta)\big)^{1-y} \right] \\ &= -\sum_{i=1}^N \Big[ y_i \log P(y_i=1 \mid X_i; \Theta) + (1-y_i) \log\big(1 - P(y_i=1 \mid X_i; \Theta)\big) \Big] \\ &= -\sum_{i=1}^N \Big[ y_i \log(\text{sigmoid}(t_i)) + (1-y_i) \log(1-\text{sigmoid}(t_i)) \Big] \\ &= -\sum_{i=1}^N \Big[ -y_i \log(1+e^{-t_i}) - (1-y_i) \log(1+e^{t_i}) \Big] \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^N \Big[ y_i \log(1+e^{-t_i}) + (1-y_i) \log(1+e^{t_i}) \Big] \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^N \Big[ y_i \big(\log(1+e^{t_i}) - t_i\big) + (1-y_i) \log(1+e^{t_i}) \Big] \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^N \Big[ (y_i + 1 - y_i) \log(1+e^{t_i}) - y_i t_i \Big] \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^N \Big[ \log(1+e^{t_i}) - y_i t_i \Big] \end{aligned}\]

where:

  • \(t_i = (\alpha+\alpha_{j_i}) + (\beta+\beta_{j_i})p_i + \theta_\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + \theta_\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{z}_{j_i} + \theta_\mathbf{m}^\top\mathbf{m}_{j_i}\) is the model’s linear term for attempt $i$
  • The sigmoids nested inside the log is expanded via:
    • $\log(\text{sigmoid}(t)) = \log\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}\right) = -\log(1 + e^{-t})$
    • $\log(1 - \text{sigmoid}(t)) = \log(\text{sigmoid}(-t)) = -\log(1 + e^{t}).$
  • $\log(1+e^{-t}) = \log\frac{t(1+e^{-t})}{t} = \log(1+e^t) - t$

The minimization operation for our model becomes:

\[\hat\Theta = \arg\min_\Theta \text{NLL}(\Theta) = \arg\min_\Theta \sum_{i=1}^N \Big[\log(1+e^{t_i}) - y_i t_i\Big].\]

Moving on, we previously modeled $\alpha_j, \beta_j$ to carry the prior $(\alpha_j, \beta_j)^\top \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \Sigma)$, thus we additionally fit them via maximum a priori. Same as our logical chain before, we also want to minimize the NLL of parameter $\Sigma$ given the observed data $\alpha_j, \beta_j$:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{NLL}(\Sigma; \Theta) &= -\log L(\Sigma) \\ &= -\sum_j \log \ell(\Sigma \mid \alpha_j, \beta_j) \\ &= -\sum_j \left[ \log \mathcal N\left(\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j\\\beta_j\end{pmatrix}; \mathbf 0, \Sigma\right) \right] \\ &= -\sum_j \left[ -\log(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2} \log|\Sigma| - \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j & \beta_j\end{pmatrix} \Sigma^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j\\\beta_j\end{pmatrix} \right] \\ &= \sum_j \left[ \frac{1}{2} \log|\Sigma| + \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j & \beta_j\end{pmatrix} \Sigma^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j\\\beta_j\end{pmatrix} \right] + \text{const} \end{aligned}\]

Similarly to $\Theta$, we can also fit $\Sigma$ via minimizing its NLL (dropping the $\text{const}$):

\[\hat\Sigma = \arg\min_\Sigma \text{NLL}(\Sigma; \Theta) = \arg\min_\Sigma \sum_j \left[ \frac{1}{2} \log|\Sigma| + \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j & \beta_j\end{pmatrix} \Sigma^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j\\\beta_j\end{pmatrix} \right]\]

To see why this shrinks low-data apartments and leaves well-observed ones alone, let us take one apartment in isolation and set $\alpha_j$ as the only variable. $\alpha_j$ gets pulled by two forces. The apartment’s own bookings pull $\alpha_j$ toward whatever value best explains them, and that pull compounds with every attempt. The prior pulls it toward 0, and that pull is fixed, set by $\sigma_\alpha^{-2}$, however much or little data exists. Thus, an apartment with a long history successfully deviates from the prior, while the prior wins by default in low-data cases. The same argument, with the same weight, holds for $\beta_j$.

We finally fold the minimization problem of two components, $\text{NLL}(\Theta)$ and $\text{NLL}(\Sigma; \Theta)$, as:

\[\hat\Theta, \hat\Sigma = \arg\min_{\Theta, \Sigma} \Big[ \text{NLL}(\Theta) + \text{NLL}(\Sigma; \Theta) \Big].\]

In practice, to keep this scalable as $N$ or $M$ grows, we can divide the whole sum by $N$ and take expectations. The loss to optimize is then given by:

\[\mathcal{L}(y \mid X; \Theta, \Sigma) = \mathbb E_i\Big[\log(1+e^{t_i}) - y_i t_i\Big] + \lambda \cdot \mathbb E_j \left[ \log|\Sigma| + \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j & \beta_j\end{pmatrix} \Sigma^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_j\\\beta_j\end{pmatrix} \right], \quad \lambda = \frac{M}{2N}\]

Notice that the first term averages over $N$ individual booking attempts, while the second averages over $M$ apartments. $\lambda = \frac{M}{2N}$ unbiasedly corrects for that mismatch, but in practice can be tuned to reflect how much we choose to trust the global norm over each apartment’s own statistics. With $\mathcal{L}(y \mid X; \Theta, \Sigma)$ in hand, we can plug it into any standard optimizer, SGD, Adam, whatever the tooling offers, and minimize jointly over $\Theta$ and $\Sigma$ over mini-batches. Alternatively, we can use EM to alternate between estimating $\alpha_j, \beta_j$ given the current $\Theta, \Sigma$, and updating $\Theta, \Sigma$ given those.

Maximizing Revenue

We’ve got a model for $P(y=1 \mid p, \mathbf{x}, j; \Theta)$. Now we use it: pick the price that makes us the most revenue, for a given apartment $j$ and a given request $\mathbf{x}$. A single booking attempt earns us $p$ if the customer accepts, and 0 if they don’t. That makes revenue a random variable, and the quantity we actually want to optimize is its expectation:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Rev}_j(p \mid \mathbf{x}) &= p \cdot P(y=1 \mid p, \mathbf{x}, j; \Theta) \\ &= p \cdot \text{sigmoid}\Big( \underbrace{ (\alpha+\alpha_j) + \theta_\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x} + \theta_\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{z}_j + \theta_\mathbf{m}^\top\mathbf{m}_j }_{c} + \underbrace{(\beta+\beta_j)}_{b} \cdot p \Big). \end{aligned}\]

To keep things tidy, fold everything not depending on $p$ into a constant $c$, and write $b = \beta+\beta_j$ for the apartment’s price slope ($b < 0$: acceptance falls as price rises). So we’re maximizing $\text{Rev}(p) = p \cdot \sigma(c + bp)$, and we want:

\[p_j^\star(\mathbf{x}) = \arg\max_{p} \; \text{Rev}_j(p \mid \mathbf{x}).\]

Taking the derivative with the usual $\sigma’(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$ identity and setting it to zero gives:

\[1 + p b \big(1-\sigma(c+bp)\big) = 0.\]

This is the classic revenue-maximization result in disguise: $pb(1-\sigma(c+bp))$ is exactly the price elasticity of the acceptance probability, so the condition says price where that elasticity hits $-1$. That maximum only exists when $b < 0$, since $\text{Rev}$ has to rise from $0$ and decay back to $0$ for an interior peak to be guaranteed, and given $b < 0$ the peak is also unique, since the algebra ahead reduces this equation to exactly one real solution.

Here $p$ is trapped inside $\text{sigmoid}$, exactly the shape the Lambert-$W$ function is built to unwind. Substituting $s=\sigma(c+bp)$ and working through the exponential algebra collapses this into a closed form:

\[p_j^\star(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{1}{\beta+\beta_j} \left[ 1 + W\!\Big(\exp\big( \alpha + \alpha_j + \theta_\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x} + \theta_\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{z}_j + \theta_\mathbf{m}^\top\mathbf{m}_j - 1 \big)\Big) \right],\]

where $W$ is the Lambert-$W$ function ($W(z)$ solves $we^w=z$). This falls out cleanly because we kept the model linear in price inside the sigmoid, a nice reminder of why linear-in-price logistic models are still everywhere in production pricing: the optimum comes for free, no numerical solver required.

In practice, this is a two-line computation (scipy.special.lambertw), but several guardrails matter more than the elegance of the formula.

  • First, clip to the observed range: the formula will happily extrapolate into prices the model never actually saw for that apartment, and logistic curves get unreliable fast outside their training range.
  • Second, check the sign of $b$ before trusting the formula at all: it only holds for $b<0$, and a non-negative fit for a given apartment means the price coefficient needs more shrinkage or more data, not a Lambert-W price.
  • Finally, the closed form only needs $c+bp$ linear in $p$, while $c$ itself can be arbitrarily nonlinear in $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}_j, \mathbf{m}_j$ without breaking anything, since none of that depends on $p$. What actually breaks it is nonlinearity in $p$ itself, like a price-squared term or a learned price-matchness interaction. If that happens, a grid search over $\text{Rev}_j(p\mid\mathbf{x})$ is a reliable, though less elegant, fallback.

The goods

With the formula and its guardrails in hand, here’s what it actually buys us. For any apartment, we can visualize the whole price curve, and get answers to the what-ifs: how much would we have needed to drop the price to keep a customer who walked, or how much more could we have charged a customer who booked anyway. Both are just other points read off the same $\text{Rev}_j(p\mid\mathbf{x})$ curve that gave us $p_j^\star$, not a separate analysis.

New apartments benefit from this setup too, for free. A listing with zero history gets $\alpha_j = \beta_j = 0$ under the prior, so it starts out priced on the population curve $\alpha, \beta$ instead of an undefined one, which is the practical payoff of the shrinkage machinery from the Modeling section.

Jaron’s third what-if, which apartment feature actually drives up the price, is sitting right there in $\theta_\mathbf{z}$ too: whichever components come out largest are the features moving acceptance, and therefore price tolerance, the most.

The gaps

All of that rests on a few assumptions, though, that can get a bit brittle in practice.

Definition of a booking attempt. Counting only submitted prices biases the data toward customers who already found the price plausible; counting every search appearance drowns it in people who never considered price at all. My instinct: the moment a specific price was shown to a specific customer for a specific apartment, the only point where accept-vs-reject is unambiguous.

Invisibility of competitor pricing. It prices each apartment in isolation, but accepting a booking is a relative decision: would this apartment beat the other tabs the customer has open for the same dates? A cheap fix: a “price percentile among comparable apartments” feature, computed per attempt rather than treated as a static apartment attribute.

Blindness to demand shocks. Holidays are calendar-predictable, worth a day-of-year feature; local events like concerts need an external feed joined by location and date; disasters can’t be featurized at all, only excluded as outliers and absorbed later through retraining. Any of these could also crash $\beta_j$, not just shift $\alpha$, since customers get far less price-sensitive once alternatives dry up, and the model has no way to tell that apart from an ordinary demand spike.

Staleness of historical data. The hierarchical prior protects against apartments with too little data, but not apartments with plenty of data that’s simply old: fifty acceptances from two years ago still look well-observed, even after the market has shifted. Fix: weight by recency, not just count, for example by down-weighting older attempts or letting $\alpha,\beta$ drift over time, which folds retraining cadence and inflation into one lookback-and-decay-rate decision.

Necessity of winning customers. If we expect to earn more from a different customer soon after, losing this one might be fine, which turns single-attempt revenue maximization into a sequential decision over a booking horizon. And for a customer we do lose, the better move might be proposing a different apartment, still the one that maximizes our revenue, rather than just letting them go, which is its own search/ranking problem.

Risk of price discrimination. If any feature in $\mathbf{x}$, like device type or search behavior, happens to correlate with willingness to pay or a protected characteristic, say, iPhone users quietly getting quoted higher prices than Android users for the same apartment, the model has no way to know that’s a problem, only that it improves the fit. Once this is setting live prices instead of describing historical ones, that risk moves from a modeling footnote to a legal one, since price discrimination on protected characteristics is regulated in a lot of places.

Wrapping up

I never actually built any of this for Jaron, it was a few hours of thinking out loud about a problem he mentioned in passing at an AI meetup. I think it answers what he asked, at least on paper: how to maximize revenue, and how to answer his what-ifs, both of which have been built into the same model. Getting it right is a different story, though. If I ever build this for real? Ask the sales manager, ask the data, that’s where I’d start, as someone without any real-estate expertise. And I’d bet on finding out how much of this I got wrong along the way.
