Text Watermarking for Large Language Models

Created on October 31, 2026

2026   ·   llms   watermarking   security   ·   ml

There’s a good chance the AI-generated text you read every day carries a hidden signature. It is embedded during generation, designed to be difficult to forge, and can even survive minor edits. Yet, it can have little effect on text quality and is virtually invisible to the naked eye.

That is the premise of statistical text watermarking: making generated text identifiable without adding a visible label. This post focuses on one such technique, the KGW watermark1. The intuition is simple: the AI model gets a subtle nudge to favor certain words over others. Let us see how a tiny bias turns into detectable evidence, and what it takes to wash it away.

A Token of Background

Before examining how text watermarking can be implemented in modern language models, let us first walk through the basic concept of watermarking and its motivation.

Text Watermarking

A watermark is a hidden signal embedded in content to provide information about its origin. In text, watermarking can help determine whether a piece of text was generated by a machine.

Early text watermarking techniques predate modern language models by decades. Some hide information in document formatting, using imperceptible changes to spacing or special Unicode space characters2. Others replace words with synonyms according to a secret key3, e.g., replacing “big” with “large”. However, these methods are fragile: formatting can disappear during text normalization, while synonym substitutions can be undone through paraphrasing. Syntactic approaches4, which restructure sentences, need a parser and carry little information per sentence.

The need for text watermarking remained relatively limited until the rise of large language models (LLMs), most visibly with the launch of ChatGPT in 2022. Since then, machine-generated text has become increasingly fluent and cheap to produce, while also more difficult to distinguish from human writing. This made spam and disinformation cheaper to automate, complicated academic integrity, and contaminated unmarked model outputs into future training datasets. Meanwhile, AI detectors that relied on writing style struggled to catch up5. It was not until August 2026 that companies became required to watermark AI-generated texts under the EU AI Act6.

These limitations motivate a different approach: rather than modifying text after it has been generated, watermarking can be incorporated directly into the text-generation process7.

LLM Next-Token Prediction

Large language models (LLMs) generate text by repeatedly predicting the next token, the basic unit of text. Depending on the model and tokenizer, a token may represent a complete word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark.

At each generation step $t$, the language model $f_\theta$ takes the conditioning context (prompt) $c$ and previously generated tokens $x_{<t}$ as inputs. Its forward pass produces a logit for every candidate token in its vocabulary $V$:

\[\mathbf{\ell}_t = f_\theta (c, x_{<t}), \quad \mathbf{\ell}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|}\]

These logits are converted into a probability distribution over candidate next tokens using the softmax function. The $i$-th token receives the probability of:

\[P(x_t = i \mid c, x_{<t}) = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{\ell}_t)_i = \frac{e^{\ell_{t,i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} e^{\ell_{t,j}}}.\]

The model then selects the next token $x_t \in V$ from this distribution via sampling. Sequentially, the full formulation for generating an output sequence of $T$ tokens can be summarized as:

\[p(x_{1:T} \mid c) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid c, x_{<t}).\]

Note that the model does not necessarily select the most probable token. Instead, it typically samples a token according to the predicted probability distribution. We can control the generation process through several tuning knobs8, including temperature (the sharpness of the probability distribution), top-k (restricting sampling to the $k$ most likely tokens), and top-p (restricting sampling to the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability reaches a threshold $p$).

Statistical Watermarking: Green light, red light

Statistical watermarking takes a different approach from traditional methods: rather than hiding explicit information in the text, it introduces a subtle statistical pattern during generation. Individually, the changes are difficult to notice, but across a sufficiently long text, they can accumulate into a detectable statistical signal. The KGW method1, introduced by Kirchenbauer et al. (2023), is a seminal example of this idea. It works by making some tokens slightly more likely to be selected than others, according to a pseudorandom rule that can be protected by a secret key.

At each generation step, KGW hashes the immediately preceding token $x_{t-1}$ to produce a seed for a pseudorandom number generator (i.e., LeftHash):

\[s_t = H(x_{t-1}),\]

where $H$ is a hash function. In private configurations, a secret key can also be incorporated into the seeding process. The seed $s_t$ determines how the vocabulary $V$ is partitioned into a green list $G_t$ and a red list $R_t$. We can control the proportion of the vocabulary assigned to the green list via a parameter $\gamma$, such that $|G_t| \approx \gamma |V|$, with the remaining tokens assigned to the red list: $R_t = V \setminus G_t$. Once the partition is determined, KGW adds a bias $\delta$ to the logits of green-list tokens, leaving red-list logits unchanged:

\[\ell_i' = \begin{cases} \ell_i + \delta & \text{if } i \in G_t, \\ \ell_i & \text{if } i \in R_t. \end{cases}\]

Softmax converts the modified logits into probabilities, and the model samples the next token as usual. Increasing $\delta$ makes green-list tokens more likely to be selected, which strengthens the watermark signal but also distorts the probability distribution more.

Next-token probabilities for an eight-token toy vocabulary, with green fraction $\gamma$ and bias $\delta$ adjustable. The green list is the first $\gamma \cdot |V|$ tokens of one context's pseudorandom ordering.

Take a toy vocabulary of eight candidate tokens with $\gamma=0.5$, so four tokens are green. With no bias ($\delta = 0$), the green tokens hold about 51% of the probability mass. As $\delta$ increases, their share rises to about 88% at $\delta = 2$ and 98% at $\delta = 4$. Increasing $\gamma$ makes the green list larger, while $\delta$ controls how strongly green tokens are favored. Even without bias, green tokens may hold more or less than $\gamma$ of the probability mass, since $\gamma$ measures their share of the vocabulary. The resulting shift in probability creates the statistical signal used to detect the watermark. Real LLMs work the same way, with vocabularies of tens of thousands of tokens.

Because the partition depends on the preceding token, it is contextual: the same token can be green in one context and red in another, rather than belonging to a fixed set of favored words. In the follow-up research9, the authors extended the method to a wider context windows of length $h$, to accept tokens $x_{t-h:t-1}$. The research also introduced alternative hashing schemes.

Statistical Detection: The greener side

To detect a watermark, the detector needs to know how the green lists were generated. This means using the same algorithm as the generator and, if a secret key was used, having access to that key.

The green-token count

To check for a potential watermark, the detector walks through the text token by token. At each position, it reconstructs the corresponding green list and adds one to the green-token count if the token belongs to that list.

Given the green fraction $\gamma$ and the bias $\delta$, the expected green-token proportion under a flat next-token distribution is

\[p = \frac{\gamma e^{\delta}}{\gamma e^{\delta} + (1 - \gamma)},\]

assuming each red token retains a weight of $1$, while each green token receives a relative weight of $e^{\delta}$. In practice, the proportion is also influenced by the LLM’s next-token distribution and the context.

Suppose the detector scores $T$ tokens and finds $n_G$ in their respective green lists. Under the simplified null hypothesis that each token belongs to its green list independently with probability $\gamma$, the number of green tokens follows a binomial distribution. To measure how far the observed count is from what chance predicts, the detector calculates a z-score:

\[z=\frac{n_G - T \cdot \gamma}{\sqrt{T \cdot \gamma (1 - \gamma)}}.\]

Without a watermark, token selection is not biased toward green tokens. We therefore expect roughly a fraction $\gamma$ of tokens to fall into their respective green lists purely by chance. When the observed count is close to this expectation, $z \approx 0$, it shows little evidence of a watermark.

In contrast, a large positive $z$ indicates more green tokens than expected by chance, resulting in a small p-value (the probability of observing a result at least this extreme if no watermark was used). For example, $z=2$ corresponds to $p\approx 0.023$. We expect a larger z-score for texts with a higher green-token ratio or for longer texts with the same elevated ratio, with the z-score growing proportionally to $\sqrt{T}$. Note that this does not tell us the probability that the text is watermarked or who wrote it.

The curve shows the expected z-score of a watermark of bias $\delta$, assuming a flat token distribution and independent positions. The p-value is one-sided and approximate, from the standard normal under the null.

Where the simple model breaks down

The binomial model helps explain the detection signal, but real text is more complicated. Token choices depend on context, and repeated phrases (e.g., “Happy birthday to you”, “def main():”) can make green-token occurrences less independent. Code and other predictable text also leave less room for the watermark to influence token choices. Detectors must also account for which tokens are eligible for scoring.

In practice, detectors use more sophisticated scoring methods and empirical calibration to account for these effects. Their performance depends on the text, its length, and the watermark configuration. Production detectors must therefore validate their error rates on representative data and choose thresholds suited to their intended use. Stricter thresholds reduce false positives but increase false negatives.

Limitations and Extensions

With the KGW LeftHash algorithm, each token’s green-list membership depends on the preceding token. As a result, the effects of text edits are local: a substitution changes the scores of the edited token and its successor, while an insertion or deletion changes the successor’s context.

Seeding on more than one preceding token (a wider context) generally makes the watermark more vulnerable to paraphrasing, although the Skip (skipping selected preceding tokens) and Min (using the minimum hash value) seeding variants retain more robustness as the context window grows9. While paraphrasing can replace many tokens, translation can change the token inventory entirely. Heavy rewriting or mixing with human-written text can also weaken the KGW-style signal.

The $z$-score against text length $T$ after random substitutions, insertions, and deletions (mean $\pm$ 1 SD across 30 runs). Longer texts can retain stronger detection signals despite editing.

Beyond passive edits and benign text alterations, targeted adversaries pose a more systematic threat. Attackers may try to remove a watermark, add one to unwatermarked text, or learn the scheme to do both. Repeated access to a generation API can reveal which tokens the watermark favors in different contexts, enabling attackers to spoof or remove it without knowing the secret key10. This is known as watermark stealing.

Text watermarking has both theoretical and practical limits. Under certain oracle-access assumptions, an attacker can remove a watermark without significantly degrading text quality, even when detection uses a private key11. In practice, short texts, editing, mixed authorship, incompatible configurations, and cross-language differences can all undermine detection. False positives are also a concern when watermarked text is rare: with a 1% false-positive rate and only 100 watermarked documents among 100,000, just 9% of positive flags would be correct, even with perfect sensitivity.

KGW is one of several approaches that trade off detectability, distribution preservation, and robustness. SynthID-Text12 uses tournament sampling to introduce a watermark while offering non-distortionary configurations. Robust distortion-free watermarks13 use keyed random sequences and edit-distance-based detection to tolerate text changes. Cryptographic schemes14 can make watermarked and unwatermarked outputs computationally indistinguishable to anyone without the secret key. Each approach offers different trade-offs, and none is robust against every form of editing or attack.

Watermarking also extends to other generative media. For images, Stable Signature15 embeds a recoverable signature in latent diffusion outputs, while Tree-Ring Watermarks16 embed patterns in the initial noise. For audio, AudioSeal17 embeds imperceptible signals in generated speech and supports localized detection. For video, VideoShield18 embeds watermarks during video generation and can localize tampering across frames and within individual frames. These methods rely on modality-specific properties and do not transfer directly to text.

Conclusion

As text watermarking becomes more widely adopted, we may need to rethink authorship. People use AI to brainstorm, draft, edit, and rewrite, making the boundary between human and machine writing increasingly blurred. A watermark might show that AI contributed to a text, but not how much, what the person brought to it, or who deserves credit. This challenges our ideas of originality and what it means to be the author of a piece of writing.

These questions extend beyond human-AI collaboration to the interactions between AI systems themselves. One model’s output may become another’s input, passing through layers of summarization, translation, and rewriting. Watermarks may offer clues about this journey, but no technical signal can capture the full story behind a piece of text.
